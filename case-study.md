# Cross-bank identity verification for online banking

## My role and scope

At a Taiwanese bank, I led a cross-bank digital identity-verification platform from design through regulatory pre-review submission.

My documented scope ended at regulatory pre-review submission, so I separate the product work I performed from the approval and public launch that followed my departure.

I aligned compliance, risk, IT, and external banking partners, and wrote the business case for the proposed service.

## The proposed service and later public journey

The service was designed to let a customer complete the identity-verification step for online-banking onboarding through a participating bank’s app and an ATM visit.

A public announcement later described customers of participating banks as eligible to apply for or reset online banking through this service.

The announcement described a customer using a chip-enabled debit card at the host bank’s ATM, receiving a QR code, and continuing verification in the app of the card-issuing bank.

The public description matters because it shows the product as a cross-bank journey: the ATM formed part of the interaction, while the customer’s own bank app completed the flow.

## The business case and its assumptions

That business case projected a 25% reduction in processing time and new fee revenue equal to 15% of ATM revenue.

These were planning estimates, not measured outcomes from a live service during my tenure.

I treat that distinction as essential to the case: the estimates explain how the proposal was evaluated, but they do not establish what the later pilot achieved.

## Reframing a budget objection

A separate internal decision centered on a proposed user-research budget for a new type of service without a close precedent.

Finance objected to the request, and I reframed the discussion around expected costs and benefits rather than the initiative’s current revenue.

I then withdrew the original request and proposed using an existing, approved open-banking budget in my department.

That gave Finance a path to agree without asking it to approve a new, unfamiliar budget line, and Finance withdrew its objection.

The lesson I took was to use a more flexible framing for a new type of business and prepare a more rigorous assessment of its expected future benefits.

This account does not claim that the projected benefits were realized, or that the budget decision by itself caused the service’s later approval or launch.

## What happened after my project work

After I left the bank, a public announcement described the service as an approved pilot and reported its later public launch.

The approval and public launch followed my departure, and the later announcement records that subsequent product history.

The announcement also described the ATM-based verification journey and participating-bank availability; it did not establish a performance result attributable to my work.

This boundary keeps the case useful as evidence of product leadership while accurately distinguishing my contribution from later operational outcomes.

## Reflection

The work required translating a new service concept into a proposal that could be assessed across product, control, technology, and partner-bank perspectives.

The business case connected the customer journey to an expected operating and revenue rationale, while the user-research budget discussion tested whether the proposal could move through internal financial review.

My strongest evidence here is the documented product scope, the business-case assumptions, and the recorded change to the funding proposal.

The public announcement gives context for the service that later reached customers, while my contribution here is evidenced by the documented project scope, business case, and funding proposal.

For that reason, this case focuses on the decisions and proposal I can substantiate, and treats post-departure service details as context only.
